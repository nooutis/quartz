## Introduction

Marx’s indisputable influence on political thought, particularly on the left, has led to a worldview that prioritises economic conditions as the driving force behind inequalities. Whilst the predominant role of economic factors is undeniable, it is clear that other factors exist which generate and perpetuate discrimination against specific social groups. The relationship between economic circumstances and social identity – be it gender, ethnicity or otherwise – is complex and remains a subject of debate, even within Marxist thought. The most reasonable approach, in my view, is one that regards the superstructure as fundamentally derived from the base, whilst acknowledging a certain degree of autonomy within it: this means that inequalities may exist which cut across the economic condition because, through a process of autonomization of the superstructural sphere, the original relations of production recede into the background and crystalize into social hierarchies, thereby altering the relationship with the economic condition. A Marxist perspective that fails to take this aspect into account risks being unable to accurately describe forms of oppression such as gender discrimination or discrimination on ethnic or religious grounds. On the other hand, it is dangerous to remove the primacy of the economic factor from the equation: the risk is that of transforming mass politics into minority identity politics, thereby alienating the working classes, who must be at the heart of any genuine left-wing politics. Institutional inequalities are not inherent to human nature: various forms of social organization have existed in which violence and inequalities were present, but these were contingent and not linked to a central authority. Although people are fundamentally different at a biological level, the difficulty in achieving substantive equality is primarily of a productive nature. A political and social system is needed that is capable of using the technical tools at its disposal to eliminate inequalities rather than exacerbate them.

## Where do inequalities stem from?

For most of history, human beings have lived in a context where institutionalized inequalities did not exist [^9].  The question of how this model broke down is therefore crucial to understanding the nature of contemporary societies. Although there is no linear, hierarchical progression, at a certain point in history there was a transition from a society in which the centralization of political power did not exist – due to the horizontal distribution of that power – to societies with a clearly defined leader. The transition from segmentary formations to centralized systems was a discontinuous process, characterized by experimentation, hybrid systems and the gradual consolidation of centralized power due to various factors, listed below: [^1][^2]

| Theoretical Reference          | Main Mechanism                                                                   | Evolutionary dynamics                                                                                     |
| ------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Administrative integration     | Centralised economy and goordination of public goods                             | Institutionalisation of power, often through hereditary succession                                        |
| Conflicts                      | Conflict between distinct political entities, territorial expansion and conquest | Well-defined changes linked to territorial factors and external threats                                   |
| Control of economic policy     | Monopoly over the flow of material resources and trade                           | Exclusion of competition by the élite                                                                     |
| Ideological and ritual motives | Construction of sacred buildings, ritual organisation                            | Oscillation between collective action and that of emerging social classe                                  |
| Multi-level cultural selection | Competitive pressure towards large-scale cooperation                             | Dissemination of military innovations through a process of centralisation of economic and political power |

Even though the emergence of hierarchies stems from multiple factors, the large-scale establishment of the state model and the near-disappearance of horizontal structures were determined above all by warlike impulses: competitive military pressure and wars of conquest made coercive centralization the only viable configuration for survival.
Hierarchies are therefore not natural, but are the complex result of a series of social, economic and cultural factors. Their existence predates contemporary capitalism, but they constitute a functional element in its reproduction and persistence.

## Hierarchies and Capitalism

In the contemporary capitalist system, there are two fundamental types of hierarchies:

- Social hierarchies
- Economic hierarchies

As seen previously, the former are contingent and independent of capitalism itself, and, whilst systemic, can be, at least in part, alleviated or reduced. Capitalism, in fact, structurally requires a certain degree of capital accumulation, inevitably generating economic inequalities. Some social inequalities, directly linked to economic power, cannot be reduced, as money translates directly into social power and is its primary expression, though not the only one; others are linked only indirectly, and to a lesser extent, to economic power, and it is therefore possible to reduce them through regulation and education: indeed, while operating within the same economic system, gender and racial inequalities have decreased in recent decades[^3][^4], despite the setbacks of recent years and the fact that we remain far from substantive equality. This is possible because, although capitalism has historically exploited the socio-economic hierarchies that preceded it, economic exploitation is the only form of oppression that is necessarily inherent in the system: other forms of exploitation are contingent and linked to factors independent of capitalism itself. Capitalism, of course, did not arise or develop in a vacuum, but its evolution can, through various adjustments, move towards a social-democratic system in which hierarchies, despite still existing, are purely economic in nature.

#### Capitalism and Patriarchy

The link between these two systems is highly complex: capitalism has supplanted the patriarchal social structure that preceded it (and which had existed for millennia across vast swathes of the planet) whilst absorbing those aspects of it that served its purposes. In particular, the gender-based division of labour has made it possible to render domestic and care work – which is essential for the maintenance of society – free of charge, without adding to the direct costs of production. It must be noted, however, that this division has not always been present under capitalism: during the period of industrialization, women, men and children were all proletarianized without distinction: the breadwinner model was merely a compromise between trade union struggles and the need to stabilise reproduction in a period that was not yet technologically advanced (mortality rates had soared), whilst now, with the automation of domestic tasks, capital is returning to its original mode of operation: treating every human being indiscriminately as a mere unit of wage labour.\
This demonstrates that gender subordination is a contingent historical configuration and that the elimination of patriarchal remnants is fully compatible with capitalist production: this would entail a shift towards a purely economic dividing line. [^10]

### Capitalism and Racism

Whilst the relationship between capitalism and patriarchy is linked to contingent situations, the relationship with racism is even more clearly so. Although capitalism has historically benefited from colonialism (and continues to do so to some extent), the slave trade and the plundering of natural resources, its development shows that racism is not a vital principle of the system, but rather a hindrance to its full realization: the logic of capital is, by its very nature, indifferent to non-economic identity characteristics, since the law of value quantifies commodities and labour solely in terms of time and money. Restricting access to the labour force or excluding a section of the population on ethnic grounds constitutes an inefficiency and a contraction of the potential market. The wage differentials that still affect minorities or populations in the Global South today do not stem from ideological factors, but from objective disparities in local economic development and from unequal legal protection, which makes certain workers more vulnerable to blackmail and exploitation. Through globalization, capital assimilates any social group into the circuit of production and consumption, provided that the conditions for profit extraction are met. The elimination of racism is therefore compatible with capitalism, despite the fact that capitalism has historically exploited pre-existing socio-economic hierarchies whenever they afforded an economic advantage.

#### The future of capitalism

In countries that have transitioned from a planned economy to a market economy, there has been a sharp rise in inequality during the transition, due to the centralisation of economic power by the elites and the loss of purchasing power among the average citizen [^5][^6]: the only way to reduce economic inequality is to act directly on the economic system. Economic inequality is on the rise [^7] and this affects everyone: an individual’s reduced economic power translates into a reduced ability to influence society and difficulties in accessing quality goods and services, a risk that is amplified for marginalized social groups, as they are more vulnerable to poverty. Historical data indicate that capital tends to become concentrated, and the only way to reduce this phenomenon is to intervene directly to prevent the formation of monopolies and break up those that already exist. The solution – following the introduction of policies for the redistribution and democratisation of credit, whilst also implementing public control in sectors where this is necessary, so as to prevent new monopolies from forming in the future – lies in a complete rethinking of the way we conceive of production: capitalism naturally tends towards the formation of monopolies [^8]. Social-democratic reforms act as a buffer, allowing exploitation and economic hierarchies to be reduced before they are eliminated entirely

## Conclusions

Social hierarchies can be significantly reduced through social democratic policies, without overhauling the capitalist economic system, whilst achieving substantive equality requires a complete rethinking of the economy as a whole. It is interesting to note that Marx’s first definition of communism is that of a society in which there is no division of labour: this echoes the original idea of human egalitarianism, characterized not only by substantive equality, but also by the absence of a rigid, hierarchical division of labour. The fundamental problem, which will be addressed in a subsequent article, is that of managing to create a society that is organized but not hierarchical.
