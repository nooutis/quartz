[[Reform or Revolution?|Italiano]]

The main goal of left-wing and far-left policies is to reduce—or completely eliminate—inequalities. [[The Roots of Inequality|While it is possible to radically alleviate gender and ethnic disparities]], it is impossible to eliminate economic disparities while remaining within capitalism; it is therefore necessary to understand how the system of production can be radically transformed. Historically, two paths have been proposed: reform and revolution. They differ in their relationship to legality and the continuity of the legal system: while reform is a process that takes place within institutions, revolution—even a nonviolent one—is extra-institutional. From a pragmatic perspective, neither path is always preferable to the other: the choice depends on historical circumstances, the political situation, and the expected outcomes. For example, revolution emerges as the only path when dealing with state apparatuses that are structurally unreformable: autocratic or authoritarian regimes in which the total absence of democratic channels and preventive repression preclude any form of gradualism, leaving the proletariat with “nothing to lose but their chains”[^2].

## A Defense of Reforms

In the history of Marxist thought, the relationship between gradualism and revolutionary rupture has constituted the main line of division. Democratic institutions and social-democratic structures can serve as a ground for political maturation. Social democracy can be an instrument of transition toward socialism when it opens up a subversive path by pursuing structural reforms capable of removing growing segments of the economy from the laws of the market, strengthening workers’ control over production processes, and sharpening class consciousness rather than numbing it [^1]. From this perspective, the reformist phase does not replace the revolution but constitutes its groundwork, exacerbating the contradictions within the democratic state until it reaches a breaking point. It follows that, where democratic space exists, parties aiming to overcome capitalism cannot take refuge in abstentionism: a presence within institutional spaces is necessary to use public prerogatives as a lever of power.

Participation in institutional spaces also has implications for political practice: the rejection of ideological purism and sectarian isolation. To influence the material balance of power and avoid marginalization, far-left parties must be able to engage in tactical compromise with democratic and bourgeois forces. Such a compromise addresses two needs: on the one hand, to seize political power in order to secure social advances for the subordinate classes, since the masses—when crushed by poverty and deprived of the material time and educational tools—are incapable of developing political consciousness; on the other hand, to exploit the internal contradictions within the ranks of the ruling class. Parliamentary compromise must not result in integration into the existing order, but must be subordinated to a strategy of counter-power and institutional tension aimed at exposing, through the practical test of events, the contradictions and limits of capitalism.

## What Makes a Revolution Possible?

Revolutions do not arise from a vacuum of discontent, but from the material contradictions of historical development: revolutionary potential is distributed in a highly asymmetrical manner. Social groups lacking structural weight in the relations of production, organizational capacity, or leverage in key sectors can only generate disjointed and failed uprisings.

For a revolution to succeed, the revolutionary class must be able to convert its economic position into political and logistical power: this is achieved by organizing the strike force of the productive bloc or by capitalizing on previously accumulated economic or political power.

This mobilization does not stem from individual utilitarian calculation, but from a collective perception of risk in which the cost of inaction outweighs the fear of repression.

However, no revolutionary front can overthrow an intact state apparatus: the fundamental condition remains a pre-existing systemic crisis that paralyzes command centers and undermines the loyalty and cohesion of the armed forces. Added to this internal fracture are international contradictions and the actions of foreign powers, capable of altering the balance of power and accelerating the collapse of the state.

### Peaceful Revolutions

Nonviolent revolution arises from conditions that differ from those of armed insurrection. Within liberal democracies, a well-organized vanguard, by targeting critical points in production and logistics, can paralyze the economic apparatus and force the state to make structural concessions. In any case, the ability to completely transform the institutional framework without armed conflict does not depend solely on the effectiveness of the blockade, but also on the ideological and political resilience of the state: when extra-parliamentary mobilization threatens the heart of the economic system, the established power typically resorts to suspending democratic guarantees and using force. Therefore, the nonviolent outcome of a revolutionary process does not stem from a unilateral choice but remains possible only if the repressive apparatus has already been delegitimized, divided, or paralyzed by a crisis of consensus severe enough to render authoritarian restoration unfeasible.

## Conclusions

There is, therefore, no real contradiction in the use of these two strategies: overcoming capitalism requires both, at different times and in different ways. On the one hand, we have legalistic reformism, which views reform as the sole means; on the other, maximalist sectarianism, which—in its messianic anticipation of the revolution—despises work within institutions and the accumulation of material power: both of these deviations have historically proven fruitless and antithetical to materialist analysis. Political effectiveness requires a flexible strategic framework, capable of adapting to the circumstances and material conditions of the state and the historical context in which one operates, and of not ruling out any instrument of action when it proves useful for subverting the balance of power.
