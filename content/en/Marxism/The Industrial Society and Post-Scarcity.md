[[La società industriale e la post-scarsità|Italiano]]

> **economy** n. f. \[from Latin _oeconomĭa_, Greek οἰκονομία, composed of οἶκος “dwelling” and -νομία “-nomia” (lit. “management of the household”)]. – The rational use of money and any limited resource, aimed at obtaining the greatest benefit for a given expenditure or the same result with the least expenditure

The fundamental core of economics is the concept of resource scarcity: resources are limited, in the sense that it is not possible to fully satisfy all of humanity’s needs. If this assumption fails, economics, as we understand it today, no longer makes sense. In reality, it is also possible to relax this requirement: it is not necessary for resources to be unlimited and needs to be fully satisfied; rather, it is sufficient for resources to be even virtually unlimited—that is, with a supply many orders of magnitude greater than demand, or with a regenerative flow that exceeds consumption—and instead of satisfying all needs, it is sufficient to satisfy essential needs: if the marginal cost of a good drops to zero, it falls outside the logic of the market. To describe a society of this kind, the terms “society of abundance” and “post-scarcity” have been coined. However, there are goods for which it is not possible to overcome the constraint of scarcity—at least for now—and for which it still makes sense to reason in economic terms, particularly time and positional goods.

## The Path Toward Post-Scarcity

Achieving a society of abundance requires two fundamental conditions:

### 1.  Automation

Technological development is a necessary [^1][^2] but not sufficient[^3] to free humankind from the trap of forced labor: without technological growth, the transition to a society of abundance is impossible and results in a state of widespread poverty. Before the advent of capitalism, human societies were effectively a zero-sum game in which economic growth was inextricably linked to population growth. It was only with the First Industrial Revolution that global gross domestic product began to grow exponentially. This created a unique effect: the concentration of wealth increased, but at the same time, the absolute wealth and income of the population rose, since the enrichment of the upper middle class was no longer intrinsically dependent on the impoverishment of the lower class. Moving beyond the zero-sum logic made the idea of class cooperation appealing: the poor and the rich can reach a situation where both sides get what they want, even if, in absolute terms, the rich get much more. However, this system cannot function indefinitely: technological growth is not a process that can last forever, at least under current conditions. Our planet has physical limits, and not all of its resources are renewable; pushing beyond these limits is dangerous for the survival of humankind: overexploitation is driving our ecosystems toward collapse—a phenomenon not new in human history, but one that is, in this case, global. Fortunately, there is a threshold beyond which the production of certain goods and services is sufficient for a stable population: human needs are not always unlimited[^4], and therefore infinite growth in production is not necessary for this purpose. Economic growth is driven by increased productivity, which means that the quantity of goods and services produced per unit of time increases. Technological growth is historically linked to economic growth, but the former could, in theory, continue even under conditions of material stagnation. Automation is a natural tendency of capitalism because, whenever possible, machines are preferable to human labor: they tend to be more efficient, do not get sick, do not need rest, and do not form labor unions.

### 2. Democracy

The wealth generated by automation does not necessarily reach workers. Another trend in capitalism is the concentration of capital and the wealth it generates[^5]; this is because, without wealth redistribution, a vicious cycle is created in which money becomes a means to obtain even more of it [^6].  Redistribution policies are therefore, initially, indispensable to ensure that technological growth benefits everyone equally and not just a tiny minority of the population. [[Reform or Revolution?|The definitive solution to this problem cannot be social democratic]], even though this model is a fundamental step toward reducing inequalities: if capitalism is maintained, the tendency toward the concentration of resources is not eliminated, but only reduced or partially curtailed: even in the most advanced Western social democracies, there is a significant wealth disparity between the majority of the population and the wealthiest 1%, which causes instability and can severely weaken the country’s democratic stability [^7]. The management of the means of production—at least in the context of essential goods, defined through an analysis of current needs to be identified at both the local and global levels via multi-level deliberative and participatory processes involving the population—must be democratic and can no longer be entrusted to the markets. The market incentivizes profit as its sole metric, which is not necessarily aligned with the collective interest. If these solutions cannot be implemented, it is possible that goods—despite having a marginal cost of virtually zero—will not be accessible to everyone because they are made artificially scarce by a private monopoly (or cartel).

## Techno-Feudalism

Failure to implement policies aimed at democratizing technological growth could have catastrophic effects: removing human beings from the economy eliminates the leverage that the subordinate classes hold over Capital. Once there are no longer human soldiers susceptible to mutiny and no longer workers organizing unions, the power in the hands of the techno-oligarchs risks becoming absolute, and capitalism risks transforming into a political system much more akin to feudalism—yet, at the same time, much more politically stable. People would continue to be oppressed—albeit in a different way than under capitalism—but in powerless roles and only to the bare minimum necessary for their subsistence. It is possible that different structures of exploitation could emerge to ensure the system’s survival: the economy does not require work that serves an actual purpose, as demonstrated—in a completely different context—by Keynes’s “potholes paradox.”

## Conclusions

Post-scarcity represents a goal worth pursuing; however, it must be accompanied by a solid emancipatory political project so that the endeavor does not degenerate into a technocratic dystopia. Technology can and must be used to advance humanity, but, as a tool, it can also be used for destructive and repressive purposes: any form of innovation must be subject to humanity, and never the other way around.
